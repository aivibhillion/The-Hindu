Supreme Court on a Sunday: stop illegal detentions, keep Metro, roads and the Internet open — “subject to law and public order”

GS-II PolityJudiciary · Fundamental rightsArticle 19 · Article 21 · Section 163Prelims + Mains

A special Sunday bench of Justices Bagchi and Mohana heard pleas over the 10 October clampdown in Delhi and issued directions to the police and the Metro authority. The court repeatedly attached the rider “subject to law and public order”, balancing protection against illegal detention with the State’s power to impose reasonable restrictions.

  • Directions: there should be no detentions except by law outside the four-km prohibitory zone in central Delhi that includes Jantar Mantar; people may assemble peacefully outside the restricted zone; barricades and Internet connectivity are to be reviewed; and a board advertising the prohibitory order outside the court itself was ordered removed.
  • Contempt notices: notice went to Union Home Secretary Govind Mohan, Delhi Police Commissioner Anurag Kumar and Delhi Metro Rail Corporation chairperson D. Thara on advocate Shailendra Mani Tripathi’s plea alleging violation of the 9 October order, which barred any outright prohibition on Metro and public transport.
  • Government stand: Solicitor-General Tushar Mehta said there was “zero reduction” in Metro service: of 303 stations, entry and exit were restricted at 45, and the Internet was restored the previous night. Justice Bagchi asked for an affidavit on whether stations at the two ends were open while middle ones were closed.
  • Petitioners: senior advocate Menaka Guruswamy asked how many were detained and whether Article 19 had been “suspended”; Gopal Sankaranarayanan said the “Emergency had reached our doorsteps”. The authorities must file a response with compliance by Tuesday.

📌 Static GK

Delhi’s second day: Connaught Place becomes the epicentre as hundreds, including an MP and journalists, are detained

GS-II PolityProtest · PolicingConnaught Place · Press freedomMains

On Sunday, 11 October, sporadic protests against the Special Intensive Revision (SIR) of electoral rolls and Chief Election Commissioner Gyanesh Kumar continued across Delhi for a second day. Shops in Connaught Place reopened after 24 hours and Metro stations shut on Saturday resumed, but barricades kept protesters away from Jantar Mantar, and the relative calm broke whenever student groups tried to march.

  • The day: around noon, 60 to 70 protesters marched from Rajiv Chowk to Palika Bazaar shouting “vote chor, gaddi chhod”; police and the Rapid Action Force detained them. By 12.30 p.m. barricades had cut off Janpath Road. About 350 people from several left-leaning groups began a rally at 4 p.m.; women activists of the Students’ Federation of India (SFI) were detained after 6 p.m. during a “Tiranga Yatra”.
  • Detained: Rajya Sabha member John Brittas of the Communist Party of India (Marxist) was injured as police dragged him to a van and was admitted to Ram Manohar Lohia Hospital; All India Students’ Association president Neha Bora was also detained. Police said protesters were “unwilling to cooperate”; protesters alleged assault.
  • Journalists: about a dozen reporters were detained at several points, including seven near Ramakrishna Ashram Marg Metro station and three from the Hindi edition of The Indian Express, who were released after about 45 minutes at Chhatrasal Stadium, nearly 12 km away. A senior police official said they may have been held due to “confusion” because they worked close to protesters.
  • Other fronts: Krantikari Yuva Sangathan activists protested outside the Supreme Court and took boats on the Yamuna, calling it the “new Jantar Mantar”. A Special Executive Magistrate directed the release of CJP leaders Abhijeet Dipke, Saurav Das and Ashutosh Ranka on condition of “no breach of peace for a year”; Mr Dipke said they had signed no bond against protesting.
  • Elsewhere: in Mumbai, police detained people near Shivaji Park, Dadar, some before they reached the site; protesters alleged excessive force.

How the Mahatma’s 1922 courtroom statement echoes in the law of dissent

GS-I History · GS-II PolityFreedom struggle · Fundamental rightsSedition trial · Article 19Prelims

The detentions of peaceful protesters on 10 October recalled Mahatma Gandhi’s statement in a colonial court on 18 March 1922, in the sedition trial over three articles in his journal Young India. The trial judge was Ahmedabad District and Sessions judge C.N. Broomfield; Gandhi and his editor Shankarlal Ghelabhai Banker pleaded guilty and were sentenced to six months’ imprisonment.

  • Gandhi’s words: “affection cannot be manufactured or regulated by law”; if one has no affection for a person or system, one should be free to express “disaffection” so long as he does not contemplate, promote or incite violence. He said non-violence implies voluntary submission to the penalty for non-cooperation.
  • Post-independence: in Himat Lal K. Shah v. Commissioner of Police, Ahmedabad (1972) a Constitution Bench held that after the Constitution the right to assemble cannot be abridged except by reasonable restrictions.
  • 2016: in Anita Thakur v. Government of Jammu and Kashmir the Supreme Court said organised, non-violent protest marches were a key weapon in the freedom struggle and that peaceful protest is a fundamental right.

📌 Static GK

The Young India trial of 1922 is also called the “Great Trial”. Gandhi was tried under Section 124A of the Indian Penal Code (sedition), now replaced by a differently worded offence in the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita. The Non-Cooperation Movement (1920-22) was withdrawn after the Chauri Chaura incident of February 1922.

UPSC 🎯 Prelims pointers

  • 18 March 1922 – Gandhi’s statement; six months sentence.
  • Himat Lal Shah (1972); Anita Thakur (2016) – protest as a fundamental right.
  • Section 124A IPC – sedition (colonial era).

Answer skeleton: Intro – Gandhi’s 1922 statement. Body – protest marches as a weapon; Art 19(1)(b); Himat Lal Shah; Anita Thakur. Conclusion – the guarantee carries the legacy of non-violent dissent.

Karnataka: about 29,000 Form 7 applications seeking deletions are missing from the portals; the CEO’s own figures keep changing

GS-II PolityElectoral roll · SIRForm 7 · TransparencyPrelims + Mains

Details of nearly 29,000 Form 7 applications, filed mainly to delete names from electoral rolls, received in Karnataka until 8 October during the Special Intensive Revision (SIR), have not been made public on the websites of the State’s Chief Electoral Officer (CEO) or the Election Commission of India, despite assurances. A statement from the office of Chief Executive Officer (CEO) V. Anbu Kumar said 1,10,654 Form 7 applications arrived between 16 June (when the pre-SIR rolls were frozen) and 8 October; The Hindu found details of only 81,822 on the Election Commission of India (ECI) website.

  • The gap: 1,10,654 minus 81,822 is 28,832, over one-fourth of the CEO’s total; without these forms it is unclear which constituencies they came from, who filed them and against whom.
  • Process: Form 7 applications received each day in each Assembly constituency should be consolidated into Form 10 and uploaded; the paper’s analysis of all Form 10 data on the portal showed the gap. The CEO’s 24 August statement had promised that lists under Forms 9, 10, 11, 11A and 11B would be available for public objection.
  • Shifting figures: the CEO’s releases gave 67,307 (to 23 August), then 34,631 (to 9 September), 41,792 (to 17 September) and 61,130 (to 24 September), before 1,10,654 by 8 October. For 16 June to 23 August the portal held details of only 6,951 of the 67,307.
  • Official response: Mr Anbu Kumar said the claims-and-objections period should be counted only from 24 August to 23 September; he was unavailable for further comment. Chief Minister D.K. Shivakumar and his Cabinet colleagues had earlier protested, after which the CEO told District Election Offices to act following an inquiry.

Mizoram to count refugees separately in the 2027 Census

Mizoram has decided to enumerate refugees living temporarily in the State as part of the 2027 Census, with their details recorded on a list separate from that of Indian citizens. The decision followed a State-level meeting in Aizawl, chaired by Chief Secretary Khilli Ram Meena, to review preparations for the Census’s second phase.

  • Numbers: Home Minister K. Sapdanga told the Assembly that 38,213 refugees were registered as of 20 August: 29,575 from Myanmar, 6,959 from Manipur and 1,679 from Bangladesh. Local organisations put the figure at over 60,000.
  • Who they are: Chin refugees came after the February 2021 military coup in Myanmar; Bawms fled a military offensive in Bangladesh in November 2022; Kuki-Zomi people fled ethnic conflict in Manipur in May 2023. All are ethnically related to Mizoram’s dominant Mizo community.
  • Biometrics and Special Intensive Revision (SIR): biometric enrolment of Bawms is complete and that of Chins reached 98.9%; people who do not wish to share Aadhaar will not be compelled. Those excluded from the rolls during SIR will be counted in the Census; 78,442 names were deleted from the draft rolls published on 4 July.

Global warming is turning sex reversal in frogs into an extinction risk

GS-III Environment · ScienceClimate change · AmphibiansAgile frogs · HungaryPrelims

Sex reversal, where an individual begins life as one sex and later switches, is well documented in animals from fish to lizards and frogs, and a warming planet is making it more frequent. In agile frogs in Hungary, researchers led by Veronika Bokony found that sex-reversed wild males sire fewer offspring, which are in turn more vulnerable to heat and infectious disease. The study was published in Science Advances on 3 September.

  • Mechanism: sex is chromosomal (XX female, XY male), but excessive heat during the tadpole stage can turn some genetic females into physically normal, fertile males.
  • Field data: from over a hundred egg masses in 16 populations, sex-reversed males made up roughly 20% of wild males but sired only 4% of clutches. Of their offspring, 61% underwent sex reversal against 17% for offspring of sex-concordant males.
  • Disease: under an artificial heat wave with ranavirus, tadpoles of sex-reversed males carried higher viral loads; ranavirus has wiped out over 90% of tadpoles in some events. Around 40% of amphibian species face extinction risk, making them the most threatened vertebrates.

What are Type I and Type II errors? — and why they matter for electoral rolls

  • Type I error: rejecting a null hypothesis that is actually true, a false positive or false alarm.
  • Type II error: failing to reject a false null hypothesis, a miss.
  • Electoral roll example: the explainer applies the idea to the Special Intensive Revision (SIR): leaving an ineligible person’s name on the rolls and wrongly leaving out an eligible voter are the two kinds of mistake. 📌 Static GK
