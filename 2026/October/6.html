Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine 2026: Deisseroth, Hegemann and Nagel for optogenetics

GS-III ScienceBiology · Nobel PrizesOptogenetics · ChannelrhodopsinPrelims

The Nobel Assembly awarded the 2026 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine to Karl Deisseroth (Stanford), Peter Hegemann (Humboldt University, Berlin) and Georg Nagel (University of Wurzburg) for discoveries that let scientists control chosen nerve cells with light.

  • How it works: light-gated channel proteins (channelrhodopsin-2 opens within 0.2 ms) are placed in specific neurons by genetic methods; light then switches those neurons on or off with more precision than drugs or electrical stimulation.
  • Uses: to study schizophrenia, depression, Alzheimer’s and Parkinson’s diseases and to test treatments for vision and hearing loss. The prize is 12 million Swedish kronor (about ₹11.5 crore), shared equally.

A protein that makes DNA without a template: does it break biology’s central dogma?

GS-III ScienceMolecular biologyCentral dogma · Reverse transcriptase · DRT3Prelims

Two papers in Cell and Science describe a new family of bacterial reverse transcriptases, DRT3 (two enzymes, DRT3a and DRT3b), where the enzyme itself seems to supply the information for a DNA sequence rather than copying an RNA template.

  • Background: Crick’s central dogma (1958) says information flows from nucleic acids to proteins but not back from protein; reverse transcriptase in retroviruses (Temin and Baltimore, 1970) was a wrinkle, not a violation.
  • Function: the DNA built by DRT3 accumulates and pushes the infected bacterial cell into dormancy, stopping the phage from multiplying. The discovery “pushes the boundaries of, but does not overturn” the dogma, says the report.
