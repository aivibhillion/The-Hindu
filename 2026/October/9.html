Serving officers of the forces have challenged the law, which affects cadre management and the deputation of Indian Police Service officers to senior posts.

  • Lead petitioner: Central Reserve Police Force Assistant Commandant Bibhor Kumar Singh, a Shaurya Chakra awardee who lost his legs in an anti-Maoist operation in Bihar in 2022.
  • Contempt angle: the Act is a key issue in separate contempt proceedings over the Supreme Court’s 23 May 2025 judgment, which directed that IPS deputation to senior posts in the forces be progressively reduced.
  • Next date: a Bench of Justices Manoj Misra and Ujjal Bhuyan will hear the contempt petitions with three writ petitions challenging the Act on 15 October.

Andhra Pradesh declares 388 mandals drought-hit after a 53.4% rainfall deficit

GS-III Agriculture · DisasterDrought · El NiñoG.O. Ms. No. 4 · 388 mandalsPrelims + APPSC

The Andhra Pradesh government has declared an early kharif-2026 drought in 388 mandals across 21 districts: 283 mandals severely hit and 105 moderately affected. It cites a 53.4% rainfall deficit in June to August and prevailing El Niño conditions. With this, more than 56% of the State’s 688 mandals (in 28 districts) are drought-hit.

  • Order: G.O. Ms. No. 4, published on 1 October by Special Chief Secretary (Revenue, Disaster Management) G. Sai Prasad after the State Drought Committee met on 30 September; it follows the Government of India’s Manual for Drought Management, 2016.
  • Rain: average rainfall was 197.6 mm against a normal 424.3 mm. The kharif declaration is normally required by 31 October; the State chose to declare early.
  • Method: three stages: mandatory indicators (rainfall deviation, dry spells); impact indicators (sown-area index, vegetation condition from remote sensing, moisture adequacy, reservoir storage and groundwater indices); and ground truthing. Rainfall deviation is “normal” from +19% to −19%, “deficient” from −20% to −59%, “scanty” from −60% to −99%; a dry spell is three to four consecutive weeks of low or no rain.
  • Next: Collectors must publish notifications in District Gazettes in English and Telugu. Data came from the Directorate of Economics and Statistics, the Agriculture and Horticulture departments, the Andhra Pradesh Space Applications Centre, Irrigation, Ground Water and Acharya N.G. Ranga Agricultural University.

Editorial: “Chance, happenstance” – why life prefers one handedness

GS-III ScienceChemistry · NobelChirality · ThalidomidePrelims

The Hindu’s editorial on this year’s Chemistry Nobel explains chirality: molecules can exist as mirror-image forms, like a left and right glove. Almost all amino acids in proteins are the left-handed “L” form, and the sugars in DNA are the right-handed “D” form; homochirality is the dominance of one handedness.

  • Why it matters: the drug thalidomide exists in two mirror forms with different shapes and effects; between 1957 and 1961 it resulted in more than 10,000 babies born with severe congenital abnormalities.
  • The laureates: Henri Kagan and Kenso Soai were awarded the prize for explaining how homochirality may have emerged and for a reliable way to make a desired variant. Soai showed that a tiny initial excess of one form can be amplified because the product catalyses more of the same handedness.
  • Lesson: small initial differences need not produce small outcomes; curiosity-driven research can yield practical benefits.

Explainer: why stubble fires keep coming back

Economist C.S.C. Sekhar (Institute of Economic Growth) explains that stubble burning is set alight on crop residue from early October to end-November across north-west India, mainly paddy straw in Punjab and Haryana, which together produce about 29 million tonnes of paddy straw. It has been practised in Punjab since the late 1970s and early 1980s after combine harvesters arrived; what changed was its timing.

  • Why now: the Punjab Preservation of Sub-Soil Water Act, 2009 bars sowing paddy before 10 May and transplanting before 10 June, pushing harvest to late October and leaving a 10–15-day window for wheat. The high-yielding PUSA-44 variety takes 150–160 days; combines leave about a foot of stubble.
  • Incentives: average paddy procurement in India was about 37% of production in 2012–24 but 87% in Punjab and 74% in Haryana; for wheat 30% nationally against 70% and 63%. Power is free in Punjab and almost free in Haryana.
  • Solutions and costs: in-situ machines (Happy Seeder, Super Seeder) need a 60-horsepower tractor and cost about ₹10,000 to hire; ex-situ uses (bio-ethanol, compressed biogas) cost ₹1,500–₹2,500 per acre. Haryana pays ₹1,000/acre for residue machines, ₹4,000/acre for direct seeding of rice and ₹7,000/acre for diversification.
  • Measurement: Punjab and Haryana reported a 90% fall in burning incidents in 2025 against 2022, but the polar satellites used observe India only between 10:30 a.m. and 1:30 p.m.; a multi-satellite estimate showed a gradual reduction of about 30%, and an ISRO-led study found farmers shifting burning to the evening (author’s account).

Editorial: the SIR, “flawed by design, exclusionary in impact”

GS-II PolityElections · Electoral rollsSIR · Lal Babu Hussein · Section 21(3)Prelims + Mains

An analysis by Srinivasan Ramani and Pon Vasanth B.A. argues that the Election Commission’s Special Intensive Revision (SIR) of electoral rolls, run under Chief Election Commissioner Gyanesh Kumar, has reversed the Commission’s historic focus on “inclusion”, and that a deferential Supreme Court judgment emboldened it (the authors’ view; the Commission’s case is not set out in the piece).

  • The numbers: 13.37 crore names were struck off at the draft stage across 30 States and Union Territories in three phases: 65 lakh in Bihar, 6.57 crore in 12 States and Union Territories in Phase 2 and 6.15 crore in Phase 3, whose final rolls are not yet published. The deletion rate rose from 8.3% in Bihar to 12.9% in Phase 2 and 17% in Phase 3 (Delhi 32.8%, Telangana 21.7%). Delhi’s draft roll has 97.54 lakh electors, barely above the 94.95 lakh who voted in February 2025.
  • Five flaws named: (1) enumeration shifted onto electors, who must trace themselves or a parent to the last revision more than two decades ago; (2) re-entry only via Form 6, so returning and new voters cannot be told apart; (3) software-flagged “logical discrepancies” such as spelling differences or parent-child age gaps outside 15 to 45 years; (4) centralised changes through the ERONet database overriding Electoral Registration Officers; (5) no Census baseline.
  • West Bengal: 60 lakh names were flagged and judicial officers deployed by the Supreme Court struck off 27 lakh; tribunals restored 93% in decided cases, but by early September had disposed of only about 1.22 lakh of more than 38 lakh appeals (3.2%). A Kolkata institute estimated 65% of those deleted for “logical discrepancies” were Muslims, who are about 27% of the State’s population (the study’s estimate).
  • The judgment: the Supreme Court upheld the Special Intensive Revision (SIR) on 27 May. The editorial says it allowed a State-wide exercise under Section 21(3) (meant for “any constituency or part of a constituency”), asked only whether the SIR was “manifestly excessive”, and sidestepped Lal Babu Hussein (1995), which struck down a sweeping citizenship-document revision in Delhi’s Matia Mahal.
  • Dissent and remedy: Election Commissioners Sukhbir Singh Sandhu and Vivek Joshi reportedly recorded at least 14 objections between October 2025 and August 2026. The authors urge the Court to revisit its judgment, halt the SIR, order an audit and return to door-to-door verification by Booth Level Officers.
