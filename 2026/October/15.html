Fifth day of SIR protests in Delhi; Supreme Court to "take care" of deleted voters; The Hindu's "logical discrepancy" analysis

GS-II PolityElectoral rollsSIR · Form 6 · ASDDPrelims + Mains

Hundreds of youth and student protesters were detained in central Delhi on Wednesday, the fifth consecutive day of demonstrations against the Special Intensive Revision (SIR) of electoral rolls and Chief Election Commissioner (CEC) Gyanesh Kumar. Youth Congress members and the Students' Federation of India and Democratic Youth Federation of India marched separately; police cited Section 163 of the Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita (BNSS). Protesters also opposed the removal of Jantar Mantar's status as a designated protest site.

  • Supreme Court: a Bench headed by Chief Justice Surya Kant agreed to intervene for Delhi voters deleted in the Special Intensive Revision (SIR) who are being asked to file Form 6 and declare they are applying "for the first time". In the Delhi draft roll 47,56,722 electors were excluded under the category ASDD (Absent, Shifted, Dead or Duplicate). Petitioners (Anjali Bhardwaj and Amrita Johri; advocates Prashant Bhushan and Neha Rathi) say a false declaration exposes them to up to one year's imprisonment under Section 31 of the Representation of the People Act, 1950; they want Form 8 allowed or a new form created. The Chief Justice advanced the hearing from 30 October to 28 October.
  • Delhi Metro: the Delhi Metro Rail Corporation told the Court that 258 of 303 stations stayed fully functional on 10 October and 32 lakh passengers travelled, against about 58 lakh on a normal second Saturday, attributing the dip to public caution.
  • Editorial, "Illogical reasons": The Hindu's investigation of Delhi's draft roll says Muslims are 13.6% of the roll but 20.3% of those flagged for "logical discrepancies" (about 30% for the five family-size reasons). In Bihar, Muslims were 18.4% of deletions against 17.7% of the population. In West Bengal, 27 lakh were deleted after adjudication, 22 lakh appealed and only 1,607 were reinstated in time; by September nearly 93% of 1.26 lakh decided appeals restored electors. The editorial asks the Supreme Court to halt the SIR (the newspaper's view).
  • Andhra Pradesh: the Election Commission of India (ECI) extended the SIR notice phase and disposal of claims and objections to 22 October; final rolls on 30 October (qualifying date 1 July 2026).

CJP leaders jailed for entering Jantar Mantar; questions over secret Section 163 orders; DMRC affidavit

GS-II PolityProtest rightsSection 163 · Section 126 BNSSPrelims + Mains

Cockroach Janta Party (CJP) leaders Saurav Das and Ashutosh Ranka were sent to five days' judicial custody for allegedly entering Jantar Mantar, which Delhi Police denotified as a designated protest site on Monday. Fourteen CJP members were detained; two First Information Reports (FIRs) were registered for trespass, disobeying orders and obstruction. A third person, Ajinkya Shinde, was released on a personal bond of 5,000 rupees with an undertaking to keep the peace for six months.

  • Sections used: proceedings began under Section 126 Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita (BNSS) (an Executive Magistrate can require an undertaking to keep the peace, up to one year) and Section 170 BNSS (arrest without warrant to prevent a cognisable offence).

Former Supreme Court judge Abhay S. Oka said Section 163(5) (a right to seek rescission) implies that orders must be public; hoardings are not enough.
  • Court: a petition by Swaty Singh challenging the denotification may be listed on 26 October; it notes the three alternative sites are on the outskirts and that no standard operating procedure for access has been issued. The Opposition plea on Special Intensive Revision (SIR) records (Election Commissioners Sukhbir Singh Sandhu and Vivek Joshi) was to be heard on Thursday.
  • Politics: Rahul Gandhi met injured students, saying protesters are "the voice of Hindustan"; CJP founder Abhijit Dipke called it an "unofficial Emergency" (statements of the named persons). GS-II PolityConstitutional bodiesAnoop Baranwal · Art 324Prelims + Mains

An op-ed by a lawyer who argued for the petitioners explains that on 23 September 2026 a two-judge Bench of the Supreme Court delivered a split verdict on the challenge to the 2023 law governing appointment of the Chief Election Commissioner (CEC) and Election Commissioners (ECs).

  • Justice Dipankar Datta: the challenge must be examined in light of the Constitution Bench in Anoop Baranwal (2023), which held that a committee with executive predominance cannot pick impartial election umpires.
  • Justice Satish Chandra Sharma: the case should go to a larger Constitution Bench. A common order asked for an expeditious hearing.
  • The law's design: a committee of the Prime Minister, a Union Cabinet Minister chosen by him and the Leader of Opposition, so the executive holds two-thirds; the law also grants lifelong immunity for acts and words in office (argued by the author).
  • Conflict of interest: the author says Justice Sharma's son has been an empanelled standing counsel for the Election Commission in the Madhya Pradesh High Court and this was not disclosed; recall petitions have been filed. He cites Rupa Ashok Hurra (2002) on non-disclosure vitiating a judgment (the author's argument).

Explained: written grounds of arrest are mandatory, says the Supreme Court

GS-II PolityRights of the accusedArt 22 · Section 47 BNSSPrelims + Mains

In Jaskaran Jeet Singh Deol v. State of Punjab (September 2026) the Supreme Court held that investigators need the magistrate's permission to re-arrest an accused whose earlier arrest was held illegal because the grounds of arrest were not supplied in writing. It recommended departmental action and a change of investigating officer.

  • Law: Article 22(2) (production before a magistrate within 24 hours) and Section 47 of the Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita (BNSS) (earlier Section 50 CrPC) require police to communicate grounds of arrest; neither prescribes the mode.
  • Cases: Pankaj Bansal (2023) – written grounds mandatory under the Prevention of Money Laundering Act (PMLA); Prabir Purkayastha (May 2024) – a vital safeguard (UAPA); Vihaan Kumar (2025) – writing ideal but not statutory; Rajesh Mihir Shah (2025) – mandatory in all offences under all statutes, in writing, in a language the arrestee understands, at least two hours before remand; non-compliance makes the arrest and remand illegal.
  • Consequences (Deol): a re-arrest application to the magistrate with reasons and a senior's endorsement, after furnishing grounds; handover of investigation; departmental inquiry; High Courts may award compensation for an Article 22(2) violation.

Why the Keynes-Kalecki v. orthodox debate matters: the economics Nobel in context

GS-III EconomyMacroeconomicsBusiness cyclesMains

This year's economics Nobel went to Robert E. Hall and Michael Woodford "for microeconomic foundations of macroeconomic stabilisation". Two economists argue the field has come full circle from Keynes and Kalecki (KK).

  • Orthodox view (Hall, Woodford): an economy follows a full-employment trend; sticky prices cause short-run slumps, so demand matters for the cycle but not the trend; monetary policy targets inflation.
  • KK view: demand affects both cycle and trend; unemployment stems from insufficient investment, not rigid prices; fiscal action and public investment fill gaps when private investment lags.
